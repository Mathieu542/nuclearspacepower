Fission reactor vs
Solar + battery in orbit

For a given electrical payload in low Earth orbit, which power architecture costs fewer kilograms to launch? This canvas compares a fission reactor (thermal conversion + radiator) against a photovoltaic array plus battery storage, as a function of altitude, required power, and the technology assumptions of each pathway. Drag the sliders — it's meant to be re-tuned. The URL updates as you go, so any scenario can be shared by link.

First-order model, basic physics only (radiative balance, orbital eclipse geometry, specific masses). No proprietary data. Layout inspired by Andrew McCalip's orbital vs terrestrial data center cost analysis — the subject and the equations here are different: mass, not cost, and power, not compute.

Mission parameters

Shared by both architectures — this is what puts them in fair competition. Start from a preset or tune everything by hand. The globe reflects the altitude and beta angle live; the marker turns orange while the satellite is in Earth's shadow.

Model assumptions

Shared

  • Circular orbit, Earth's shadow modeled as a cylinder (no penumbra).
  • Launch mass only — no hardware cost, no non-recurring development cost here.
  • No fairing envelope or launch sequencing constraints.
  • No radiation degradation of structures, other than solar cell degradation.
  • No deorbit, on-orbit servicing, or regulatory compliance mass accounted for.
  • Power management & distribution (regulation + distribution downstream of the power source) is charged at the same specific mass to both architectures — it's a common problem once electricity exists, not specific to how it was generated.

Technology-specific

  • Nuclear: reactor core is sized by thermal power, conversion equipment by electrical output — so the efficiency slider correctly moves both waste heat and reactor mass. Specific powers are treated as constants (no scale effect — a future refinement). No reactivity loss over mission life.
  • Nuclear: shield mass is a fixed input, not derived from thermal power or altitude — a known simplification.
  • Nuclear: radiator sized purely from radiative balance (Stefan-Boltzmann) at the chosen hot-side temperature; no conduction/interface gradient modeled.
  • Solar: the array must power the payload in full sun and recharge the battery before the next eclipse — sized for steady periodic operation.
  • Solar: linear cell degradation; array is oversized at beginning of life (BOL) to hold average end-of-life power.
  • Solar: no dedicated radiator mass — assumes the array backside is sufficient, as on a Starlink-class bus.
  • Solar: deployed panel area is illustrative only (areal power density × array power) and does not feed back into the mass; its slider range shifts with the chosen array specific power to stay within real space-array technology.
